# Yellow Wallpaper

by Charlotte Perkins Gilman

"The Yellow Wallpaper" by Charlotte Perkins Gilman is a short story published in January 1892. This landmark work of feminist literature and horror fiction follows a woman confined to a nursery by her physician husband as treatment for "temporary nervous depression." Forbidden from working or writing, she documents her experience through secret journal entries. With nothing to occupy her mind but the room's disturbing yellow wallpaper, she descends into madness, becoming obsessed with its strange patterns and the figures she perceives within it.

Is there anything of literary consequence in the wallpaper descriptions?

Yes. The descriptions are where most of the story’s craft lives.

She describes it like a critic, and diagnoses it like a doctor. Her first take is aesthetic: “committing every artistic sin,” with talk of radiation, symmetry, and “the principle of design.” Gilman had art-school training, and it shows. Then the vocabulary turns medical: “debased Romanesque with delirium tremens,” “fatuity,” “lame, uncertain curves.” John diagnoses her, and she diagnoses the wallpaper. It’s the only patient she’s allowed.

The descriptions are her forbidden work. She’s barred from writing and from thinking about her condition, yet the paper “provokes study,” and she studies it for hours. All the intellect John wants switched off goes into analyzing a pattern. That’s why these passages are her most articulate writing: they are the one sanctioned outlet for an unsanctioned mind.

The words fit her as well as the paper. Curves that “suddenly commit suicide,” a neck that “lolls like a broken neck,” a pattern that confuses and irritates and won’t resolve. She is describing herself without knowing it. The woman behind the pattern only makes literal what the language was already doing.

The register degrades with her. The descriptions move from design criticism, to organic imagery (fungus, toadstools, wallowing seaweed), to faces and eyes, to bars, and finally to a “yellow smell” that leaks out of the room and through the house. The analytic distance shrinks until the paper is no longer something she looks at but something she’s inside.

You can’t picture it, and that’s deliberate. Try to draw this wallpaper from the text and you’ll fail. It goes diagonal, horizontal, radial, and none of them at once. The reader ends up repeating her task, trying to make the pattern resolve, and feeling a small dose of the same frustration.

The horror is domestic. It’s not a ghost or a crypt. It’s mass-produced decor in a room “for” a woman, repeating “by the breadths.” The ordinary furnishing of women’s lives is the monster.

So the wallpaper isn’t scenery. It’s the narrator’s mind, her confinement, and the story’s method, all at once.
